01 The problem

What am I working with?

Cooper Lighting Solutions had built its office and hospital business on two older systems, Fifth Light and iLight. Both were being retired. WaveLinx DALI was the replacement: a new, internet-connected lighting platform for large buildings. Its first job was the Peter Gilgan Mississauga Hospital, the biggest installation of its kind in North America.

There was nothing to improve on, because there was no product yet. And the technology underneath came with rules I could not touch. DALI-2 is an international standard for how lights, sensors and switches talk to each other over a shared pair of wires. It decides what has to happen, in what order, and how long each step takes. You cannot negotiate with it. The design had to live inside it.

4,000+ The smallest number of devices on a typical hospital job. At full size it passes 10,000

40 runs One controller can carry up to 20 hubs, and each hub feeds four cable runs

128 max The most one cable run can hold: 64 lights, plus 64 sensors and switches

The real problem was time

The wires are slow by design, and no amount of effort was going to change that. The first version of our software made it worse. Adding a single hub froze the entire app behind a message reading “Processing! This may take a while... Adding DALI Hub and Devices to WAC” and it stayed there until every last device had been found. That wait started at ten minutes. Engineering later stretched it to 27 and a half minutes so it could cope with 250 devices. Now multiply that by ten hubs on one controller. The person doing the work is standing in a hospital corridor, doing nothing.

What went wrong in version one

The moment you added a hub, the software went off and searched all four cable runs at once, and locked the screen while it did it. No moving around, no naming rooms, no getting ahead. On a hospital job that meant electricians standing still for ten minutes or more per hub, with no sense of how far along it was, no way to stop it, and nothing useful to do in the meantime.

What was riding on it

This was the system replacing Fifth Light and iLight. The contractors, integrators and hospital facilities teams who would live with it every day were watching to see whether it could really take that work over. How it felt to set up was the thing that would win or lose the deal. A frozen screen at hospital size was not something anyone was going to accept.

02 My role

Taking the lead

I owned the design for the whole set-up experience, which was the main design story on the project. I led it from the first research session through to the final handoff, working shoulder to shoulder with engineering, architecture, product management, firmware and development. Every milestone was presented to rooms of 25 or more people, to gather feedback and keep everyone pointed the same way.

What I owned

The whole set-up journey: finding hubs, the Lobby, managing a hub, putting devices into rooms, and the floor plan view · The version one and version two flows · A custom icon set, drawn in Adobe Illustrator and packaged as a font · The printed label that goes on the hub itself, ready for the press · Design reviews across every team · New pieces for the Cooper Lighting design system in Figma · Follow-up after launch

The people I worked with day to day: two engineering managers, two product owners, two product managers, one architect and five developers. I sat with the firmware engineer who wrote the code that scans the wires, and turned what the hardware could and could not do into something that made sense on a screen.

The constraint I could not move

Scanning is slow because the standard says so. Giving 128 devices their own number and asking each one what it is takes real time. This was never a problem engineering could solve; the ceiling is physical. The design had to absorb the wait rather than pretend it wasn’t there.

03 Getting started

Learning the ground before drawing anything

Research and design happened at the same time, because the hardware was still being built and the firmware was still being written. So I used the engineering team as my main source while the physical system took shape, and filled in the rest with stories from the field and a close look at how the competition does it.

  • Long sessions with the firmware engineer, learning exactly what happens when the system finds and numbers devices, in what order, and why each step takes as long as it does
  • Reading every document on the project and mapping how the pieces fit together: the kinds of device, and how much one controller can actually hold
  • Pulling apart the old systems, Fifth Light and iLight, to find the habits installers would expect to bring with them
  • Looking at three competing systems already running in hospitals and offices
  • Early workshops to find the bottlenecks before a single screen was drawn, stress-testing the whole application rather than only the new part

How the research worked

Getting everyone in the room first. On a project this technical, design research means closing the gap between what a designer knows and what the people building the hardware know. So the work started with structured conversations across five groups, before anything was drawn.

  1. 1. Engineering and firmware

    Told me how much traffic the wires can carry, how long it takes to find a hub, and how often the app could check in without slowing down the job going on around it.

  2. 2. Developers

    Warned me that dropping 3,000 devices into the existing list would time the app out and take the rest of it down with it.

  3. 3. Architecture and product management

    Explained how a hospital job actually runs, in what order, and the rules that apply in places where wireless is not allowed.

  4. 4. The people who do the set-up

    Described the job on the floor: a paper plan in one hand, a list of hardware IDs in the other, working one cable run at a time, and needing to know which light is which straight away.

  5. 5. Hospital facilities teams

    Told me what happens after we leave: new devices added as wings get built, hubs that need restarting without taking a live floor down, and faults that have to be found fast.

What one of them told me

“I’ve been doing DALI for 12 years. I know what order things need to happen. But your app doesn’t, so I have to fight it.” Master electrician, research participant

The finding that changed everything

The most important thing to come out of research was that the existing WaveLinx software had been built for 30 to 50 devices per controller. Every list, every room screen, every map quietly assumed that. DALI-2 was not a feature you bolt on. It forced us to look again at how the entire application copes with volume.

A custom set of icons

Every one of these devices shares a single physical trait: a two-wire connection. I made that the backbone of the icon set. The same two-wire mark sits inside every icon, so you can spot this family of devices instantly and still tell the types apart. I drew them all in Adobe Illustrator, tuned them to stay readable at small sizes, exported them as SVG, and checked them in Fontello so they render the same way everywhere.

Four things that shaped the design

One: the wait was a design problem before it was a technical one

The scan was never going to get faster in time for launch. But version one made it hurt far more than it had to by freezing everything behind it. The opening was not to speed the scan up. It was to stop making people watch it. The set-up work and the scanning had to come apart.

Two: start with the hub, not the devices

Early sketches let you add devices while the hub was still being found. Talking it through with engineering showed that was backwards. You add a hub; the devices come with it. Leading with the hub made the whole thing easier to picture, cut the number of decisions at the most uncertain moment, and matched what the hardware was really doing underneath.

Three: one light on the ceiling, two things on the screen

Some lights can shift the color of their white, from warm to cool. Physically that is one unit on the ceiling, but the software has to treat it as two: one part for brightness, one for warmth. People see one fitting and find two entries in the app. That gap had to be smoothed over in the design, or people would set them up wrong.

Four: the sheer number of devices would break what we already had

The existing room screen was built for a sensible number of wireless devices. A hospital floor running six or more hubs could throw thousands of lights, sensors and switches at it at once, filling the screen and grinding it to a halt. The way people move around the app had to be rethought from the ground up.

How we worked, week by week

Weekly reviews across every team were the engine of this project. Each round followed the same shape.

Weeks 1 to 2 Draw the flow for one problem, then take it to engineering to check it can actually be built

Weeks 3 to 4 Put prototypes in front of product management and architecture, to make sure the order of the work is right

Week 5 onward Review with the whole group, firmware and development leads included, every single week

That rhythm shortened the loop dramatically and kept the design moving in step with engineering. It mattered most when the timings changed, because what the screen could honestly promise depended entirely on how long the hardware really took.

04 The turning point

Throwing out version one

Halfway through, it was clear version one could not be patched. The wait was baked into its shape. Moving to version two was not a new coat of paint. It changed the order of the entire job.

Version one, dropped

Look for hubs and devices at the same time. Adding a hub locks the screen. Nothing else is possible. You wait out ten minutes or more, per hub, and then start working.

Version two, shipped

Look for hubs only. One button pairs the hub and starts the search for devices quietly in the background. The screen never freezes. You start naming rooms and grouping lights straight away, while the devices arrive behind you.

The whole change in one sentence

We stopped designing for the moment the search finishes, and started designing for everything you can get done while it is still running.

05 Design

Finding the hubs and setting them up

Fast, and clear about what it has found

Setting up a hospital means working through hundreds of hubs and thousands of devices, with a paper floor plan you keep checking against hardware IDs on the screen. The flow had to be quick, hard to get wrong, and honest at every step about what the system had found so far.

06 Design

Looking after the hubs

The years after the building opens

Setting up is only the first day. Hospitals grow, floors get rearranged, and new devices get added to cables that are already live. The Manage Hub screen covers the rest of that life: checking a hub is healthy, adding what is new, and putting things right without disturbing a working floor.

Why it works this way

Keeping new devices separate from existing ones, before anything is committed, was a deliberate safety catch. In a building this size, accepting an unexpected pile of devices creates problems that take days to unpick. The markers let you triage at a glance and decide which run to deal with first. The five to eight minute estimate went in because the people doing the work asked for it: they need to know how long a live hub will be busy so they can plan around it.

07 Design

Putting devices into rooms

Coping with the sheer number of them

The existing room screen was built around a modest number of wireless devices. DALI-2 broke that assumption completely. A hospital floor running six or more hubs could drop thousands of lights, sensors and switches into the unassigned list at once, filling the screen, stalling the app, and making it nearly impossible to find the one device you actually wanted.

The fix: choose first, load second

I put a filter above the unassigned list: which system, which hub, which cable run. Instead of loading everything and hoping, you say where you are working, and the list narrows to a single run and its 128 devices before anything is drawn at all. An empty state reading “Select Filter to Start” makes that deliberate, and quietly prevents the accidental load-everything that would have taken the app down.

Why it works this way

Choose first, load second was the central decision here, a deliberate move away from show-everything. It is not only faster. It changes how the job feels. It nudges people to work one cable run at a time, which is exactly how the building is wired and how the work gets handed out on site. One run at a time means fewer mistakes, a screen you can read, and an app that matches the paperwork in your hand.

08 Design

The floor plan

Rebuilding how you get around, for thousands of devices

The floor plan was built for a world where one floor had 30 to 50 devices. DALI-2 made it 3,000. The old panel down the left side would have meant endless scrolling, painful load times, and no realistic way to find one specific light on a live hospital floor while somebody waits. The whole way you move around it had to be rethought.

Out with the tree, in with menus

I replaced the fixed panel with a row of menus across the top: building, floor, area, zone, device. Nothing loads the whole tree up front. You pick your way across, each choice narrowing the next, and only what you need gets fetched. You can also jump straight from a building to one room without walking through every level in between.

Grouping the dots so the map stays readable

Put hundreds of devices on one floor plan and the markers pile on top of each other into an unreadable smudge. So nearby devices bunch together into a single dot with a number on it. Zoom in and the bunches split back into individual devices; zoom out and they gather again. The map stays readable, and fast, at every level.

Why it works this way

Swapping the tree for menus was the biggest structural change in the whole project. It turns the screen from a list you browse into a map you use: the floor plan becomes the main thing, and the menus become a way of narrowing down rather than a ladder to climb. With grouping and type filters on top, the same map serves a small office and a hospital floor without behaving any differently.

09 Looking back

Designing while the ground moved

Nothing on this project stood still. Hardware prototypes turned up a few at a time. Whether the third-party devices would even be available was an open question all the way through. So I worked in quick prototypes to pressure-test ideas fast, and reviewed them weekly with the whole group so the design never drifted away from what engineering had just decided.

Each stage of the project, what it threw at us, and what I did about it
Stage What happened What I did
Dropping version one Engineering said the frozen screen would not survive at this size. A design review with the development team laid the wait problem bare. The version one spec had a ten-minute freeze built into it. Started version two. Changed the order of the job from “search, then set up” to “add the hub and set up while the search runs behind you.” The search ended up taking 27 and a half minutes, and now nobody has to watch it.
Managing a hub, in three steps The first version of the Manage Hub screen was one long, undifferentiated list. The field services and engineering teams said it was unclear how to search again or how to add what was new. Broke it into three steps: search and see what is new, look at what was found and check which device is which, then add them. Every step got a confirmation and a way out.
Too many devices for the floor plan The old floor plan structure would have crawled at this size. It also became clear the problem was bigger than the new feature; the whole app needed testing under load. Ran a stress test across the entire application. Brought in the menu navigation, the grouped dots, and choose-first load-second to handle 3,000 devices and more.
Color tuning lights that count as two Where a room has only one white-tuning zone, these should pick it automatically. Where there are several, people need a way to choose for each of the two parts. Engineering had to confirm what was possible before I could specify it. Wrote the rule for the automatic case and designed the pattern for the rest, matching how the existing two-channel product already behaves.
Names and icons Five different kinds of device sitting in the same lists needed consistent names and icons before the testing team could write anything they could actually test against. Built the custom icon set around the two-wire mark, and set the naming for every type of device. Confirmed that the older and newer versions of the same fitting look identical on screen, because to the person using it, they are.

It outlived the project

The patterns from this work, the search that never blocks, the two-banner progress model, choose-first load-second, menu navigation, and how a device and its endpoint get shown, are now the reference for every future system that gets plugged into WaveLinx. They live in the Cooper Lighting design system, so nobody has to solve them twice.

11 My impact

Why this one needed me

This is not a case study about making a hard screen easier to read. It is about designing a product that did not exist yet, on top of a standard nobody could change, while the hardware, the firmware and the design were all being built at the same time, and where getting the shape wrong would have shipped inside a certified, hospital-grade system at the largest installation of its kind in North America.

Dropping version one meant recognizing that a ten-minute freeze was not a loading-screen problem to be prettied up. It was a mismatch between the way people expect the job to go and how long the technology actually takes. Engineering’s answer was to make the timeout longer, out to 27 and a half minutes. The design answer was to make the entire wait disappear by building around it. Getting there took enough understanding of the technology to know exactly what could and could not be changed.

The limits of the wiring, the naming, the one-light-two-channels problem, replacing the floor plan structure: none of these were design calls made on their own. Each one meant holding engineering constraints, product promises, testing dependencies and hardware availability in view at the same time. Years spent in connected lighting, in enterprise software, and around hardware gave me the standing and the vocabulary to lead those calls rather than wait to be told.
