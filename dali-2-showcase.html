The short version

One system, three jobs

Every light, sensor, and wall switch on a floor is joined by the same two wires. Three people work with it: an electrician puts the wires in, a setup person switches it all on, and a building team looks after it for years.

Why it matters: a hospital can have 4,000 to 10,000 lights, all on wires so they never get in the way of medical machines. So even a tiny annoyance, times thousands, turns into days of extra work.

Step 2 · Switch it on

From "sit and wait" to "just keep working"

Switching on a floor used to mean staring at a loading screen. Adding one box froze the whole app while it read every device. That took up to 27 and a half minutes, and there were ten boxes. Our fix wasn't a faster wait. It was making the wait something you never have to sit and watch.

The floor map

Works for a few lights or thousands, same map

One dropdown lets you go from the building, to a floor, to an area, to a room, down to a single light. The map bunches lights into groups on its own. Zoom in to split a group apart, or filter by type. It's the same map everywhere.

Pick one wire before it loads · lights grouped when zoomed out · single lights when you zoom in
How we knew

We watched people use it

Before we launched, we sat with real users and gave them real jobs on the real screens: find and add a box, look after it, and sort lights into rooms.

What we asked them to do
"Add this box."
"Rename it. Did the labels change everywhere?"
"Put this light in this room."
"Put a whole wire's lights into one group."
"What do these running counts tell you?"
What we learned
A new name has to show up everywhere.

People expected a rename to change every screen at once, so now it does.

What we learned
A floor can be a thousand things on one screen.

So we show one wire at a time before anything loads, and bunch the map into groups until you zoom in.

Proof

Then we tried to break it

On a test bench wired up just like a real building, we tried more than 200 kinds of device on all four wires: dimming, changing colour, wall-switch scenes, and reacting to daylight, across dozens of rooms. Almost everything passed. A few just needed parts that hadn't arrived yet.
